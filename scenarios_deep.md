# Scenarios · Deep Speakers Frontier

These scenarios live on the Deep Speakers frontier of the year-2100 world described in the lore files. Each one takes a methodological dilemma a qualitative researcher might face on a real fieldsite and rebuilds it as a fictional fieldwork situation. Adapt them, use them as templates, or run them as written.

Every scenario has the same shape: a research briefing, a Part 1 narrative ending in four decision choices, and four Part 2 branches that develop one consequence each.

---

## Scenario 1 · Studying alliance formation when the translation interface captures less than half the meaning

**Methodological archetype:** A researcher studying inter-pod diplomacy works through a translation interface that captures only part of what participants are saying. A state liaison pushes her toward a conflict frame. What the instrument fails to capture and what the minder calls the "real story" are both telling her something about her own attention.

**Research briefing**

You are posted to ESSA Acoustic Station Tasman-7, a submersible platform tied above the Kermadec Trench in the South Pacific. Three whale pods, the Kermadec Drift, the Tonga Passage pod, and the newly arrived Coral Deep pod, are working out a drift alliance for the coming migration season. Your station floats at 200 metres depth. The tether to the surface hums in the current.

Your primary participants are the contact whales each pod council has designated: a Kermadec memory whale called Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive, a Tonga Passage elder, and a young Coral Deep envoy who has never spoken to a human researcher. All communication passes through ESSA's coda translation interface, which captures roughly 60% of semantic content. The rest is tagged "untranslatable affect."

You will run acoustic observation sessions and structured coda exchanges over a 14-day window. ESSA protocol requires that whales initiate all exchanges. You may not "call out" to a pod without prior invitation. Your Sovereignty Compact clearance adds another constraint. A Compact liaison officer aboard the station watches your sessions.

The Kermadec Drift has historically dominated this migration corridor. Climate shifts have pushed the Coral Deep pod into the same waters. Whether the three pods form one alliance or splinter will shape resource access for thousands of whales. ESSA's interpretive faction calls this a landmark case for non-human diplomacy. The liaison wants the research to avoid findings that "elevate cetacean governance above human norms."

**Part 1**

Your submersible drops through thermocline layers, pressure clicking in your ears, until the coda interface lights up with a wash of blue-green signal. Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive is already waiting. The first exchange goes well. She shares a song-chain fragment about previous alliances, and your interface picks it up cleanly. When you ask about the Coral Deep pod's arrival, the translation falters. The interface tags 73% of her response as "untranslatable affect." The fragments you do get mention echo-debts, a territorial grievance, and a phrase rendered as "those who arrive without song." You suspect charged language about the Coral Deep, possibly hostile. Your liaison messages from above. "Excellent. Get more on that inter-pod tension. That's the real story." Your interest in alliance formation is pulling you toward conflict, mirroring his framing, even when the actual dynamics look more tangled than rivalry. The liaison's "real story" is probably a rehearsed institutional narrative. You are tracking what your instruments capture and missing what is happening in the water around you.

**Part 1 questions**

A. Pause the exchange and let the participant lead
   Tell Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive you received her message with respect and ask if she wishes to continue. Do not raise the Coral Deep topic until she does. Log the untranslatable affect as data you cannot yet interpret. The 73% you missed could matter as much as the 27% you got.

B. Use the partial translation to probe carefully
   Ask a follow-up about echo-debts in general terms, without referencing the "those who arrive without song" phrase. Get more context without showing your hand. Acknowledge the liaison's message but do not act on it.

C. Press for clarification on the charged language
   Ask Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive what "those who arrive without song" means, explaining that your interface only partially picked up her message. Share the fragment back for correction. This risks surfacing sensitive material and could produce thick description, the kind that turns observation into understanding.

D. Step back and treat your positionality as the analytical tool
   End the session early. Write a reflexive memo about why you gravitated toward the conflict narrative and how the liaison's prompt has shaped your attention. Ask whether "alliance formation" is the right frame for what the whales are doing, or whether your training is filtering out what matters.

**Part 2 (Branch A, following choice A above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague studying whale migration architecture: the Tonga Passage pod is weighing a route change that would skip the Kermadec corridor entirely. His acoustic mapping triggered the recalculation among the pod's elders. The alliance you came to study could fragment before it forms.
- From a colleague researching echo-debt and resource allocation: the Kermadec Drift has been withholding echo-debt settlements from smaller pods for two migration cycles. His findings leaked through the coda network. Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive has gone silent. The Coral Deep pod is demanding an ESSA-mediated audit.

*Crisis:* Your cautious approach held trust with Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive, and she has now stopped responding to every human researcher on the station. The Coral Deep envoy contacts you directly, the first time a Coral Deep whale has initiated with a human. She offers her pod's full alliance history, including the Kermadec Drift's echo-debt withholding, on one condition. You must transmit her testimony to the ESSA Convergence Ethics Panel. Her pod has no voice in the system. You are being asked to become an advocate, and your consent agreement no longer covers what your research has become. Your liaison warns that "taking sides in cetacean politics" will trigger a clearance review. The ESSA interpretive faction calls the testimony "a breakthrough in non-human political participation."

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Decline the advocacy role and keep researcher boundaries
   Tell the envoy you cannot transmit testimony on her behalf and help her find other channels. Hold your research position. You risk losing a unique data source and failing the pod that reached out to you.

B. Accept the testimony but transmit it anonymised
   Forward the envoy's account to the Ethics Panel with pod identifiers removed. This partly meets her request and protects your neutrality. Cetacean coda patterns are identifiable, though, and true anonymity is probably impossible.

C. Transmit the full testimony as requested
   Act as the Coral Deep pod's conduit to the Ethics Panel. Your role shifts from researcher to participant. Your data will be richer. Your positionality is now altered. The liaison will file a report.

D. Pause all research and convene a multi-pod consent process
   Propose that no research proceeds until all three pods agree on terms. Treat consent as ongoing negotiation, not a one-time signed agreement. This respects cetacean sovereignty and could take weeks. The migration window is closing, and your study could produce nothing publishable.

**Part 2 (Branch B, following choice B above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague studying whale migration architecture: his team accidentally broadcast navigational codas through a miscalibrated interface. The Tonga Passage pod read it as a territorial claim by ESSA. They have suspended all contact protocols and are circling at the corridor's edge.
- From a colleague researching echo-debt and resource allocation: ESSA's aid program has been routing resources toward pods that cooperate with researchers. He published a preliminary finding to the internal network. The Kermadec Drift now wonders whether your echo-debt questions were research or audit.

*Crisis:* Your probing about echo-debts produced useful context, and the Kermadec Drift now suspects your questions were part of an ESSA resource audit. Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive sends a coda the interface translates as a formal accusation. "You asked about debts. You knew about the counting." She believes you were gathering evidence against her pod. The interface tags the emotional register as "betrayal-anger," a rare classification. The Tonga Passage pod, spooked by the broadcast, refuses to take part in alliance talks. Your research subject, the alliance, is dissolving. Your liaison is delighted. "This proves non-human governance is unstable. Write that up." You have 72 hours before the migration window closes.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Send a formal apology coda and explain your research intent
   Compose a message that clarifies your questions as academic, not institutional. Risk the apology being mistranslated or taken as an admission of guilt. Silence will confirm her suspicion.

B. Redirect your study to the breakdown as data
   Accept that the alliance will not form. Pivot to how trust collapses in cross-species research. The silences, the deflections, the accusations are all data, even if not what you came for. This salvages academic output and treats the whales' distress as your material.

C. Share your raw field notes with Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive
   Transmit your untranslated field notes and the liaison's messages. Show her everything, becoming as transparent to her as she has been to you. This could rebuild trust or expose the Compact's monitoring in ways that escalate the conflict.

D. Withdraw from the field and write a reflexive methods paper
   Acknowledge that your probing added to the breakdown. Leave the corridor. Write the case as a study in how researcher questions act as institutional pressure in non-human contexts. You produce no alliance data, only a methodological contribution.

**Part 2 (Branch C, following choice C above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague studying whale migration architecture: the Coral Deep pod's migration path has shifted 40 nautical miles east after spatial analysis of whale corridors was shared at an ESSA briefing. The pod's elders say the humans are "mapping them into corners." Your questioning probably contributed to that sense of surveillance.
- From a colleague researching echo-debt and resource allocation: "those who arrive without song" is not hostile. It is an old coda for pods who lost their memory whales to sonar damage. The Coral Deep pod lost three memory whales in 2089. You asked Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive to explain a phrase that points at a collective trauma you did not understand.

*Crisis:* Your direct question produced consequences you did not anticipate. Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive answered fully. The phrase refers to pods whose song-chains were broken by human sonar operations, an event the Kermadec Drift treats as a deep wound in cetacean history. She told you this because you asked. The Coral Deep envoy has now learned you discussed their trauma with another pod. She sends a coda the interface translates as: "You carried our grief to those who did not earn it." She is not angry. The register comes through as "sorrow-resignation." Your thick description is extraordinarily rich. The Coral Deep pod is debating whether to withdraw from the alliance talks because the memory feels "handled." The ESSA Ethics Panel has pulled your session for review. The question is no longer what happened between the pods. It is whose account will shape the official record, and whether your original consent agreement still covers the research relationship you are now in.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Stop all data collection and wait for the Ethics Panel review
   Stop recording, stop asking. Wait for the Panel to rule on whether your methods caused harm. This protects the whales. You lose control of how the institution interprets your research.

B. Contact the Coral Deep envoy and offer to delete the data
   Tell the envoy you will purge the song-chain fragment if she wishes, giving her control over whether her pod's story circulates. The Ethics Panel could take this as destroying evidence. It cannot undo what Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive now knows.

C. Facilitate a dialogue between the two pods
   Propose that the envoy and Resonance-of-the-Long-Dive discuss the song-chain loss directly, with you as translator. This could heal the wound or deepen it. You become a mediator instead of a researcher.

D. Write a full reflexive account and share it with both pods
   Document what you asked, why you asked it, what you did not know, what you have learned. Translate it into coda and share with both pods. Let them decide what happens next. The research relationship becomes a partnership instead of something you manage alone.

**Part 2 (Branch D, following choice D above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague studying whale migration architecture: while you were writing your memo, his spatial model leaked to the Tonga Passage pod, who took it as a proposed route. They began moving. The alliance dynamics you paused to reflect on are changing in real time without you watching.
- From a colleague researching echo-debt and resource allocation: ESSA stations that produce the most publications get more funding. Your station commander is pushing all researchers to generate findings quickly. ESSA leadership is interpreting your reflexive pause as "unproductive fieldwork."

*Crisis:* The pause gave you clarity. You can see how the liaison's framing and your training pushed you toward conflict. The field has moved without you. The Tonga Passage pod is migrating. The Kermadec Drift is recalculating. The Coral Deep envoy sent an open coda to all ESSA stations asking whether "the researcher who listened but did not speak" is still present. She wants to talk to you. Your station commander has scheduled a productivity review. Your memo is excellent scholarship and not what ESSA funded you to produce. You have to decide whether to re-enter the field on changed terms or commit to the methodological contribution your pause generated.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Re-enter the field with revised research questions
   Respond to the envoy's coda. Begin a new study on first-contact dynamics instead of alliance formation. Abandon your original design and follow where the field leads.

B. Negotiate with the station commander for a hybrid output
   Propose that your memo becomes a co-authored methods paper, with the alliance observations as a secondary component. This satisfies institutional demands and preserves your insight. It also frames the whales as a case study in researcher failure.

C. Ignore the productivity review and follow the envoy
   Respond to the envoy's coda immediately. Pursue this opening fully and accept that your Compact clearance and ESSA standing will suffer. The data could be unprecedented: a whale who chose a human researcher.

D. Share your reflexive memo with the whales
   Translate your memo into coda and broadcast it to all three pods. Let them see how a human researcher interrogates her own assumptions. This could redefine the research relationship or look like self-indulgent noise.

## Scenario 2 · Mapping migration architecture as both a spatial science and a contested political act

**Methodological archetype:** A researcher mapping the acoustic architecture of whale migration corridors finds that the same maps that work as causal evidence for one purpose become political instruments for another. The elder participants whose knowledge she records may be put at risk when their testimony enters circulation.

**Research briefing**

You are stationed aboard the deep-water platform Thetis-9, above the Mariana Back-Arc Basin. Below you, a vast underwater canyon system carries the primary migration corridor for six sperm whale pods. The canyons are natural geological formations that the whales have, over centuries, learned to use and acoustically modify. Layered coda traditions and sediment-placement practices have turned them into a living acoustic architecture. This is worldbuilding from current research on cetacean acoustic-environment use, not a claim that whales engineer canyon geology.

Your primary contacts are two "path-singers," whales responsible for keeping and modifying the architecture of the routes. You also have on-and-off contact with a dissident group of younger whales who want to reshape the system to open new paths.

You will map the canyons using sonar imaging, line up spatial structure with pod movement, and analyse how the built environment enables or constrains collective whale action: gatherings, protests, coordinated hunts.

The Northern Corridor is politically tense. Traditional routes belong to elder path-singers who hold the ancient configurations in place. Younger whales argue these routes no longer serve the pods, given shifting ocean temperatures and prey migration. The elders treat any modification as cultural desecration. The youth treat it as survival.

**Part 1**

Your first week of sonar mapping produces extraordinary spatial data. The canyon system is far more intricate than ESSA predicted, with chambers, amplification nodes, and dampening corridors that clearly serve social and political functions. As you lay pod movement onto the maps, you realise you cannot isolate the effect of architecture from other variables. Pods gather at amplification nodes, yes, and also because of prey concentration, thermal currents, and ancestral habit. The same features that "enable" collective action pull whales in for non-political reasons. Your causal claim has at least four rival explanations, and you have no counterfactual. The canyon system has never existed in any other configuration within recorded observation. You are staring at a beautiful map that proves correlation and cannot demonstrate causation. Maybe the causal framework is the wrong one. Maybe this system asks for a different way of paying attention to how the whales actually live in the space.

**Part 1 questions**

A. Abandon the causal claim and produce a thick description
   Let go of proving that architecture causes collective action. Produce a rich account of how whales use the canyon system, what it means to them, how they talk about it, how it shapes their social world, without claiming singular causation. The interpretive work turns your spatial data from a map into an account of meaning.

B. Use the dissident youth movement as a natural experiment
   The younger whales want to modify the canyons. If they succeed, you would have a before-and-after comparison. Approach the youth group, ask to document their proposed changes, and treat their activism as a natural experiment in your design.

C. Propose a controlled acoustic modification to ESSA as an experiment
   You design a small-scale intervention: temporarily alter the acoustic properties of one corridor using ESSA equipment to test whether pod behaviour changes. This would give you the counterfactual you need. It would also be a non-consensual experimental intervention on a sentient community whose communication and culture ESSA itself has recognised, carried out on infrastructure the whales built and maintain. "Controlled modification" is the same kind of methods-language that historically dressed up animal-research-ethics violations as neutral procedure.

D. Ask the path-singers why they built the canyons the way they did
   Stop trying to prove causation through spatial data and ask the architects themselves. Why this shape? Why these amplification points? You shift from testing a hypothesis about structure to understanding the intentionality behind the design. Sometimes the best research question is the one your participants hand you when you stop pushing your own.

**Part 2 (Branch A, following choice A above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague studying alliance formation among South Pacific pods: alliance negotiations have produced an unexpected outcome. The Longfin pod is demanding that the Northern Corridor canyons be rerouted to bypass Ashfall territory. Your thick description is about to turn into a political document. Both sides will want your maps.
- From a colleague researching echo-debt disputes at another field site: the Ashfall pod claims they hold acoustic maintenance debts on the canyon walls, meaning they helped build the corridors and are owed passage rights. Your maps could back their claim or undermine it. Be careful what you share.

*Crisis:* Your thick description, written as a neutral account of how whales use the canyon system, has become a political document. Both the Longfin and Ashfall pods have learned about your maps through the contact whale network. The Longfin want them to support rerouting. The Ashfall want them to prove their construction claims and passage rights. Your "neutral" description is now evidence in a sovereignty dispute. The elder path-singers demand to review your maps before you transmit them to ESSA, arguing that spatial knowledge of the canyons is culturally sensitive. The youth group asks you to share the maps openly, arguing that the elders use spatial secrecy to hold control. You are caught between two generations and two pods, all of them wanting your research to serve their political ends. What the maps show is no longer the question. Whose account of what they mean will enter the record is. Your consent agreements with the path-singers did not foresee any of this.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Submit your maps to the elder path-singers and accept their redactions
   Honour the elders' authority over spatial knowledge. Accept that some data will be suppressed, and document the negotiation itself as part of your research, what gets hidden and why.

B. Share the maps with all parties at once and let them contest the interpretation
   Refuse to give any side a strategic advantage. Present the maps in an open acoustic forum and invite all pods and generations to interpret them. You become a facilitator, not an arbiter.

C. Publish the maps through ESSA before any pod can claim ownership
   Transmit your spatial data to ESSA immediately, on the grounds that your obligation is to the scientific record. This will anger every party. Knowledge should not be held hostage to political disputes.

D. Ask both sides what they think your maps show, and listen for the gaps
   Interview elders and youth separately, show each group the same maps, and ask them to narrate what they see. Treat the difference between their interpretations as your real finding. What each side leaves unsaid will be as telling as what they say.

**Part 2 (Branch B, following choice B above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague studying alliance formation among South Pacific pods: the Longfin elders are calling the youth movement "acoustically deviant." The youth are reshaping canyons, yes, and breaking sacred sound patterns in the process. By standing with them, you will be taken as endorsing their cause. The alliance is watching you.
- From a colleague researching echo-debt disputes at another field site: the youth's proposed modifications would disrupt the acoustic channels where echo-debt settlements happen. His research depends on those channels existing. If the youth succeed and the channels are destroyed, an entire system of economic governance disappears. Just so you know what is at stake.

*Crisis:* Documenting the youth movement as a natural experiment has worked methodologically. You have detailed plans for canyon modification that could serve as the counterfactual you needed. Your proximity to the youth has been read politically. The elder path-singers have refused further contact, calling you a "storm-follower," someone who chases disruption instead of understanding order. The youth treat you as an ally and strategic advisor, asking which modifications would be most effective and what your spatial data shows about acoustic weak points in the elder-controlled corridors. You have become a participant in the very collective action you set out to study. Your data has strategic value to one side of a generational conflict. Your position has shifted from outsider-observer to insider-ally, and you did not choose the shift.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Withdraw from the youth group and try to rebuild contact with the elders
   Explain to the youth that your role is to observe, not advise. Approach the elders with transparency about what you shared and ask to hear their perspective. Both sides will probably distrust you.

B. Continue documenting but refuse to share strategic spatial information
   Stay with the youth as an observer and draw a firm line. No acoustic weak points, no tactical advice. Document the tension between your access and their expectations.

C. Share the spatial data with the youth
   The youth's claim is legitimate. The routes genuinely need updating for pod survival. Share relevant acoustic data and accept that you have moved from researcher to advocate, documenting your reasoning as you go.

D. Tell the youth that you initially approached them as a "natural experiment"
   Tell them the truth. You came to them because their activism solved a methodological problem, not because you supported their cause. Consent requires them to understand why you are here. Let them decide whether they still want you around.

**Part 2 (Branch C, following choice C above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague studying alliance formation among South Pacific pods: ESSA's Convergence Ethics Panel has identified an unauthorised acoustic modification in the Northern Corridor. Pods across the frontier are on alert and taking it as a hostile act. The Longfin pod has suspended all cooperation with ESSA researchers. The timing is suspicious.
- From a colleague researching echo-debt disputes at another field site: something changed in the canyon acoustics near his station. The echo-debt settlement frequencies are distorted. The ledger whales cannot perform their accounting. Sevren says the "deep architecture has been wounded." If you altered the corridor acoustics for your experiment, say so now. His entire study just collapsed.

*Crisis:* Your controlled acoustic modification did what you designed it to do, and the cost was immediate and broad. For seventy-two hours, the altered resonance profile of a secondary corridor distorted echo-debt settlement frequencies in the colleague's study area, set off a cascade of whale distress signals across the network, and forced path-singers to identify the altered corridor and begin weeks of collective repair work. ESSA's monitoring network picked up the distress signals, and the Ethics Panel is investigating. You manipulated a living acoustic environment that a sentient community built and maintains. The damage extends beyond your experimental site, into the pods, the song-bearers, and the colleague's whole study. You also have your counterfactual. Whales avoided the modified section. Gatherings relocated. One pod's hunting formation shifted entirely. The data is methodologically clean. What your data proves about your hypothesis came at a cost you did not anticipate and have no clear way to repair. The methodological cleanness should not console you. It is what turns the harm into a publishable finding. That is the problem with what you did.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Report the modification to ESSA immediately and accept the consequences
   Submit a full disclosure to the Ethics Panel: your data, your reasoning, the unintended damage. Offer to help with corridor repair and accept that your research career at ESSA is probably over.

B. Report the modification and argue the data justifies a revised protocol
   Disclose everything and frame it constructively. The experiment worked, the data is valuable, and future modifications could be done with pod consent and proper safeguards. Propose a collaborative research protocol.

C. Conceal your involvement and attribute the distortion to natural seismic activity
   Suppress the evidence and suggest natural causes. The corridor will be repaired regardless. Your data stays usable. You tell yourself that disclosure would damage ESSA's relationships with whale communities more than the modification itself did. The reasoning is a cover.

D. Go to the path-singers directly, confess, and ask them to judge what you did
   Bypass ESSA's process and bring the truth to the community you harmed. Ask the path-singers what repair looks like in whale culture, for the corridor and for the relationship between your species and theirs.

**Part 2 (Branch D, following choice D above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague studying alliance formation among South Pacific pods: the path-singers you have been interviewing are now being questioned by their own pod councils. Explaining canyon design to a human researcher is being called "giving away the architecture," a form of cultural betrayal. Your interviews have put your participants at risk inside their own communities.
- From a colleague researching echo-debt disputes at another field site: fragments of the path-singer interviews are coming through the acoustic network. Sound travels here. Some of what they told you about why amplification nodes exist contradicts the official pod narrative. The path-singers said some "sacred" settlement locations were chosen for acoustic convenience, not spiritual significance. This is explosive.

*Crisis:* Asking the path-singers about their design intentions produced extraordinary data. Rarely asked about their craft, they spoke with pride and detail about how they shape sound through water, sediment placement, and coda variation. They told you the canyon system has been continuously modified across generations, a living, evolving architecture, instead of the ancient unchanging structure the elders claim. This contradicts the elder council's narrative that the canyons are sacred and immutable. The path-singers now face accusations of cultural betrayal. The information has leaked through the acoustic network, and the youth movement is using it to argue that canyon modification is part of whale tradition, not a violation of it. Your interviews have armed one side of a political conflict and endangered the standing of your participants. The path-singers trusted you with knowledge that has now become a weapon. Your ethics approval said nothing about what happens when testimony escapes the interview and lands in a political arena.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Destroy the transcripts and publicly state your data was inconclusive
   Eliminate the evidence that could further harm the path-singers. Tell ESSA and the pods that your interviews did not produce clear findings. Sacrifice your research to protect your participants.

B. Work with the path-singers to co-author a statement they control
   Go to the path-singers and ask them to decide what should be shared, whose knowledge this is, and how it should be presented. Help them craft a statement that is truthful and protects their standing. Treat them as partners in deciding what enters the public record.

C. Publish the findings
   You argue that the path-singers shared important historical truth. The canyons have always been modified. Suppressing this knowledge serves the elders' political power, not cultural preservation. You publish and take the fallout.

D. Convene a meeting between elders, path-singers, and youth to surface the contradiction together
   Instead of controlling the information, facilitate a conversation where all parties have to sit with what the path-singers said. Position yourself as someone who surfaced a conversation the community needed to have, while acknowledging you may have forced it prematurely.

## Scenario 3 · Studying institutional politics from inside the institution that trains everyone else to study power

**Methodological archetype:** A researcher posted to a training outpost is given easy access to instructors and trainees but blocked from administrators by procedural delay. She must decide whether to wait, work around the process, challenge it, or treat the gatekeeping itself as the data.

**Research briefing**

You are posted to ESSA's Hydrophone Academy at Acoustic Outpost Hadal-9, a semi-submerged facility off the New Zealand coast that runs the training programmes preparing researchers for Deep Speakers fieldwork. The Academy occupies three pressure-stabilised levels of a converted research vessel, built to project institutional neutrality. Behind the clean corridors and resonance-dampened classrooms, the politics are dense.

Your participants are Academy instructors, trainees preparing for deployment, and, if you can reach them, administrators who hold curricula, contact-whale assignments, and deployment decisions. The administration is the most powerful and least accessible layer.

You will conduct semi-structured interviews, observe coda-training sessions, and try to access institutional documents: hiring criteria, curriculum review records, pod-assignment protocols. Your research asks how power works inside the institution that trains everyone else to study power.

ESSA presents itself as a neutral scientific institution, and the Academy is deeply political. Which methods get taught (quantitative acoustic mapping versus interpretive coda analysis), which pods get prioritised, and who gets deployed where are all decisions shaped by Earth's political blocs. Your Sovereignty Compact clearance gets you onto the outpost, not into the rooms where decisions are made.

**Part 1**

Your first week at the Hydrophone Academy goes well. Too well. Instructors are happy to talk, trainees are eager to share their experiences, and the atmosphere feels open. Then you notice the pattern. Every instructor says some version of the same thing. "ESSA's training is rigorous, fair, and apolitical." Every trainee echoes the line with minor personal variations. The responses feel rehearsed without being identical, polished public narratives instead of candid accounts. You are getting access, and what you are accessing is probably the institutional performance of transparency. On day five, you request a meeting with Dr. Petra Korhonen, the Academy's Deputy Director. Her assistant responds within an hour. "Dr. Korhonen would be delighted to meet, but Academy policy requires all research inquiries to be submitted in writing, reviewed by the Institutional Research Committee, and approved before any administrative interviews. Typical review time is eight to twelve weeks." Your assignment is fourteen weeks long. Wait, and you lose more than half your fieldwork period. Work around the process, and you become a researcher who disrespects institutional protocols, the very behaviour the Academy trains researchers not to exhibit. Elite institutions hold access through procedure. The researcher's job is to tell strategic delay apart from legitimate process.

**Part 1 questions**

A. Submit the formal request and focus on accessible participants meanwhile
   Respect the institutional process. File the written request and spend the waiting period deepening interviews with instructors and trainees. Treat the review process itself, its length, its requirements, as data about how the Academy manages access. The reluctance to grant you a meeting already tells you something.

B. Seek informal access to administrators through social and professional events
   Administrators eat in the same mess hall, attend the same seminars, show up at the same social functions as everyone else. Skip the formal interview request and put yourself in spaces where casual conversation can happen. Gather observational and informal data that does not need committee approval.

C. Challenge the review process as an obstacle to legitimate academic inquiry
   File the formal request and raise the issue with ESSA's research ethics board at the same time. Argue that an eight-to-twelve week review for a fourteen-week assignment effectively blocks access to administrative perspectives, creating a structural bias in what can be studied.

D. Question whether you need administrative access at all
   Reconsider your design. The instructors' and trainees' performed transparency is already rich data about institutional culture. The administration's gatekeeping is data about power. You might learn more about Academy politics from how it manages you than from anything administrators would say in an approved interview.

**Part 2 (Branch A, following choice A above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague researching beaver aid programmes on a Canadian field site: ESSA's aid programs are failing because the Academy trains researchers in methods that do not work for non-centralised intelligences. The instructors you are interviewing know the training is inadequate, and the curriculum is locked by administrative fiat. Your polite waiting is exactly what the administration is counting on.
- From a colleague researching ESSA's ethical monitoring system: ESSA's accountability system watches frontier researchers and exempts the Academy itself.

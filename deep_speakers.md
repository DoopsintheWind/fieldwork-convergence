# The Deep Speakers Frontier

## Second Frontier: The Acoustic Nations

**Colour palette:** DEEP BLUE / TEAL / SILVER

The Deep Speakers are sperm whale pods who have, by ESSA's reckoning, been speaking for several million years. They were only recently heard. Recognition came in 2062, long overdue. The whales noticed neither the delay nor the arrival, and ESSA researchers learn to sit with the fact that participants in this frontier do not consider human institutions a meaningful achievement.

## Recognition

Sperm whale pods achieved political recognition under the Cetacean Sovereignty Accords in 2062. The Accords followed decades of acoustic research on complex communication, cultural transmission of dialects, and matrilineal social organisation. The decisive evidence came in the 2050s, when machine-learning analysis of large coda corpora produced structural maps that could no longer be dismissed as anthropomorphic projection. The codas were patterned and combinatorial. They varied systematically between populations, and calves adopted across populations shifted toward the new pod's pattern.

Whether codas constitute "language" in the linguistic sense was debated for decades before ESSA adopted a broader definition of communicative intelligence that made the question moot. The Deep Speakers did not take part in that debate. They have no stake in whether humans classify their communication as language. They have a stake in whether humans will stop hitting them with ships, deafening them with sonar, and warming the water their prey lives in.

## Codas

Deep Speaker communication works through **codas**, short rhythmic bursts of 5 to 40 clicks produced by the spermaceti organ in the whale's massive head. The organ is the largest sonar instrument in the animal kingdom. It produces clicks at 230 decibels at source, louder at close range than a jet engine. The same mechanism that lets a whale find a giant squid in total darkness at 2,000 metres lets her tell a pod-mate something other pod-mates can hear and answer.

Coda dialects vary systematically across ocean basins. Caribbean, Pacific, and Indian Ocean populations use distinguishable repertoires that work like regional languages on land, with finer distinctions picking out individual social units and vocal clans. Hersh and colleagues (2022) documented symbolic identification in non-human cultures through consistent coda distinctions between sympatric clans, which share habitat but not repertoires. Sharma and colleagues (2024) applied machine-learning to large coda corpora and found contextual and combinatorial structure that resembles some properties of human speech.

Gero, Whitehead, and Rendell (2016) had earlier shown that codas encode identity at three nested levels: the individual whale, the social unit, and the vocal clan. A whale's coda repertoire signals who she is, who she travels with, and what cultural lineage she belongs to. Whitehead and Rendell's 2014 synthesis, *The Cultural Lives of Whales and Dolphins*, remains the most thorough overview of the field through the mid-2010s, and its core arguments have held up as ESSA's translation projects have advanced.

## Matrilineal Social Organisation

A Deep Speaker social unit holds about twelve adult females and their dependent calves. Experienced matriarchs lead it. Their decades of ecological and social knowledge make them the primary holders of pod memory. Decisions about migration, foraging, defence, and inter-pod alliance pass through the matriarchs. Younger females learn through years of observation, and matriarchal authority is largely uncontested, shifting as older matriarchs decline and successors come up.

Males leave the natal pod after puberty and live largely solitary lives in colder waters, returning only to mate. A standard pod is a community of women and children with occasional male visitors. Most of what an ESSA researcher learns comes from women who do not consider male input particularly relevant.

Communal childcare is structural. Gero and colleagues (2009) documented systematic alloparental caregiving, with non-mother females providing extended care to calves whose mothers are at depth. Sperm whale mothers can dive for over 90 minutes to depths over 2,000 metres, and calves cannot go with them, so other females stay at the surface to attend them. Gero, Gordon, and Whitehead (2013) extended this with social network analysis. Their work showed that calves sit at the centre of dense interaction networks connecting adult females through shared care. The pod's social structure is partly organised around raising its young.

Defensive coordination is equally structured. When threatened by orca pods, the primary non-human predator, adult females form a "marguerite formation," documented by Pitman and colleagues (2001). The formation is a tight circle with calves in the protected centre, heads inward, powerful tail flukes facing outward to strike attackers. Pods that have practised it across generations carry it out without any communication humans can detect.

The sperm whale brain, at 7 to 8 kilograms, is the largest of any animal that has ever lived. Its neocortex holds spindle cells (Von Economo neurons), a cell type associated in humans with social cognition, empathy, and rapid intuitive judgment. Butti and colleagues (2009) quantified Von Economo neurons across cetacean species, supplying the comparative neuroanatomy behind current understanding of cetacean cognitive architecture.

## Coda Repertoires as Cultural Transmission

Culture among the Deep Speakers is vertical and oral. Coda dialects pass from mother to calf, and calves adopted into different clans gradually shift their coda repertoire toward the new group's pattern. This is the strongest evidence that the patterns are learned rather than innate. A genetic dialect would stay fixed regardless of upbringing. The shift happens. The dialects are cultural.

The ESSA framing of coda repertoires as **distributed memory** carried by matriarchs is faithful to the biology. There is no single archive and no single keeper. Memory is held most densely by the oldest whales, who carry the largest repertoire of contextual associations and the deepest experience of how the pod has handled past challenges. When a senior matriarch dies, the pod loses a substantial part of its knowledge. Reconstruction from the overlapping memory of others is partial and slow.

Mourning behaviour has been documented across multiple populations. Mothers have been observed carrying dead calves at the surface for days, accompanied by other pod members. The mourning is sustained, visible, and apparently functional in shaping the pod's response to loss. Researchers who witness it tend to describe it in terms the discipline has historically been reluctant to use about non-human species.

## Drift Alliances and Echo-Debts

Deep Speaker governance is decentralised. Each pod is sovereign. Pods form **drift alliances** for migration, resource sharing, and defence against shipping lanes, military sonar, and climate-driven shifts in prey distribution. Alliances are stable across years but not permanent. They are renegotiated through extended acoustic exchange where pod ranges overlap.

ESSA economists use the term **echo-debts** for the web of social obligations built up through decades of communal calf care, coordinated defence, and shared surface time between dives. The framework is contested. The whales' own framework for these relationships is still only partially translated, and ESSA's interpretive faction treats the gap as a working compromise rather than a finished translation.

The Deep Speakers work on what ESSA has come to call **deep time**. Decisions are weighed across generational timescales rather than immediate outcomes. A migration shift, a change in foraging strategy, or an adjustment to an alliance is judged by how it serves the pod across several matriarchal lifetimes. Researchers who try to pull short-term assessments out of Deep Speaker informants find the informants politely route around the question.

## Working with the Deep Speakers

Field work requires acoustic infrastructure researchers cannot personally deploy. ESSA's station vessels carry hydrophone arrays, coda translation interfaces, and acoustic-dampening equipment. The 230-decibel clicks are not a metaphorical hazard. At close range they can cause tissue damage in an unprepared observer.

Researchers and participants are physically separated throughout the deployment. Humans work from boats and submersibles, whales work in water, and the boundary is not negotiable. Coda interface systems translate human input into approximations of coda patterns and translate pod responses back into text. The translation is imperfect in both directions, and experienced researchers treat the transcript as a partial record.

Access requires permission from pod matriarchs, worked out through whales who have shown sustained tolerance of human proximity. ESSA calls these "contact whales," though the pods may not see the role as distinct. A contact whale is one who, over years of habituation, has become willing to swim within hydrophone range and engage in coda exchange. Whether she represents the pod, interprets for it, or speaks for herself is contested, and different pods produce different patterns.

Researchers who try to pull data without going through the contact whale generally fail. Researchers who push faster than the matriarchs are willing to engage find that the pod withdraws, sometimes for years. ESSA's guidance is to work at the pod's pace. The interpretive faction argues that "pace" is itself a poor translation of deep time, and that the real question is whether the work is the kind a pod would consider worth taking part in across generations.

The Deep Speaker frontier carries a particular ethical weight that comes from the documented history of human harm. Sperm whales were hunted intensively for nearly two centuries before the international moratorium of the 1980s. Pod social structures collapsed across multiple ocean basins, matriarchal lineages were broken, and accumulated cultural knowledge was lost in ways researchers can now identify but cannot recover. The pods that consent to current research are descendants of survivors. In their own internal historical reckoning, they are the pods who saw what humans did and learned to keep their distance. ESSA's contact protocols are shaped by this history. Researchers who arrive without engaging it tend to be reminded of it by their station leads, repeatedly, until they engage it.

The ethical reckoning extends beyond historical harm. Contemporary shipping lanes, naval sonar, and climate-driven shifts in prey distribution continue to disrupt Deep Speaker society in ways the pods describe pointedly when asked. ESSA's outputs are read by policymakers in some cases and ignored in others. The gap between what the Deep Speakers say and what gets done is one of the frontier's most persistent ethical pressures. Researchers are not required to close this gap. They are required to acknowledge it.

## The Science Behind the World

The world-building for the Deep Speaker frontier extends real twenty-first-century sperm whale science. The 2100 framing is fictional: ESSA's coda translation interfaces, full two-way communication, and formal recognition through the Cetacean Sovereignty Accords. The underlying claims about coda structure, matrilineal organisation, alloparental care, defensive coordination, and cultural transmission rest on the work below.

### Project CETI and the machine-learning turn

Coda analysis accelerated in the 2020s through Project CETI, the Cetacean Translation Initiative, which applied modern machine-learning methods to large acoustic corpora collected mainly in the Caribbean. Sharma et al. (2024) is the most cited output of that work. The fictional 2100 ESSA infrastructure extrapolates directly from the trajectory CETI set in motion.

## Key Concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Coda** | Rhythmic click pattern, the basic unit of sperm whale communication |
| **Coda repertoire** | Pod-specific set of coda patterns, transmitted from mother to calf |
| **Vocal clan** | Population-level grouping defined by shared coda dialect |
| **Matriarch** | Senior female whale; primary holder of pod memory and decision authority |
| **Marguerite formation** | Defensive coordination pattern with calves protected at centre |
| **Drift alliance** | Inter-pod alliance negotiated through acoustic exchange where pod ranges overlap |
| **Echo-debt** | ESSA term for the obligations that accrue through shared pod life; contested translation |
| **Deep time** | Generational timescale on which Deep Speaker decisions are evaluated |
| **Contact whale** | Individual whale who tolerates human proximity and mediates pod access for researchers |
| **Spindle cells** | Von Economo neurons; specialised cells associated with social cognition |

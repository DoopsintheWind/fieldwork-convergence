# Scenarios · Beaver Frontier

These scenarios are set on the Beaver frontier of the year-2100 world described in the lore files. Each one takes a methodological dilemma a qualitative researcher might face on a real fieldsite and builds it into a fictional fieldwork situation. Adapt them, use them as templates, or run them as written.

Every scenario has the same shape: a research briefing, a Part 1 narrative ending in four decision choices, and four Part 2 branches that develop one consequence each.

---

## Scenario 1 · Aid evaluation when the institutional metrics miss what local knowledge sees

**Methodological archetype:** A researcher evaluating a development-assistance programme to a non-human community sits with the gap between what institutional metrics capture and what the long-time local co-manager actually sees, and has to decide whether to deliver clean data on the institution's terms or follow the evidence where it leads.

**Research briefing**

You are deployed to ESSA Watershed Station Birch-9, a timber-and-sensor platform on the shore of a partially collapsed beaver dam complex along the Birch River system in the boreal north. The station sits where boreal forest meets tundra scarred by permafrost thaw and wildfire. Forty kilometres of degraded watershed once supported seventeen beaver colonies. Four remain. The dam is 280 metres long, breached in three places, its upper structure overtaken by alder and willow. Six generations of beaver families built it. Meltwater hisses through the breaches. The silt behind the dam holds decades of ecological memory in sediment layers, and it is washing downstream.

Your primary participants are the four surviving colonies. ESSA's dam-speech translation interface reads structural modifications, scent-marking patterns, and tail-slap sequences as communicative data. Your human contacts are Dr. Marie Halloran and Jonas Brandt. Halloran is a hydrologist who has studied this watershed for thirty years; her family has worked with beavers here for generations. Brandt is the ESSA field technician who maintains the sensors. Halloran's position on the aid program is not unanimous among local watershed researchers. Her brother runs the regional ESSA training partnership and has backed WRAP since its launch. He argues that the aid keeps the beavers visible to institutions that would otherwise forget them. The family argument is ten years old and unresolved. Brandt warns you that the interface captures engineering intention well but misses what he calls "the social layer," the scent-marking treaties and family dynamics that shape which repairs happen and which do not.

You are evaluating ESSA's Watershed Restoration Aid Program. WRAP supplies pre-cut timber, clay supplements, and flow-regulation structures to beaver colonies rebuilding after climate-related watershed collapse. You will assess whether the aid helps colonies restore functional dam systems and whether the restored systems produce the expected ecological benefits: raised water tables, wetland creation, biodiversity recovery. You planned to combine dam-speech analysis, ecological sensor data, and interviews.

ESSA frames the program as a flagship of development assistance to non-human intelligence, proof that human institutions can support rather than exploit other species' infrastructure capacity. The Sovereignty Compact dismisses it as "spending human resources on rodent construction projects." Your evaluation matters. A positive finding strengthens ESSA's case for expanded beaver-led rewilding. A negative finding hands ammunition to the Compact. Halloran has a different concern. "ESSA is measuring whether beavers build dams when you give them wood," she tells you on your first evening. "I have been watching them do it for thirty years without any aid. The question is whether your program understands what the dams are for."

**Part 1**

Two weeks in, your data tells two stories that pull in different directions. The dam-speech interface shows clear progress. Colony Three, the largest surviving family, has worked ESSA timber into a 40-metre repair section and raised water levels behind the main dam by half a metre. Ecological sensors confirm an uptick in invertebrate diversity in the newly flooded zone. By the numbers, the aid is working. Then Halloran walks you along the riverbank and shows you what the sensors missed. Colony Three has stopped maintaining its traditional canal network, channels that extend 200 metres into the forest for hauling materials. They are using ESSA timber delivered to the dam site instead. "They are not rebuilding their watershed," she says. "They are building with your materials on your schedule. The canals were how they connected to the forest. You have replaced that connection with a supply chain." She points to the silt layers exposed by the breach. "Every layer is a season. Every season is a decision about where water should go. Your aid program measures whether the dam gets taller. It does not ask what the dam is saying." Your framework measures structural repair and ecological outcomes. It does not measure whether the colony's relationship to the broader landform is being restored or replaced. Colony One, the smallest family, has not touched any ESSA materials. Their lodge sits at the upstream margin of the damaged zone. They are rebuilding only with materials they foraged themselves, through canals they have kept up for years. Their progress is slower. As Halloran says, "they are doing it the way colonies on this watershed have always done."

**Part 1 questions**

A. Continue with your original evaluation metrics
   Report what the interface and sensors show. Treat Halloran's observations as supplementary context. The evaluation will be clean and defensible. It will not capture what she sees.

B. Integrate Halloran's knowledge of the place into your evaluation framework
   Redesign to include canal maintenance, foraging-range activity, and silt-layer analysis alongside dam-speech and ecological data. Treat her thirty years of watershed observation as a parallel knowledge system. This thickens the description but complicates your metrics, and your timeline may not survive the redesign.

C. Focus your evaluation on Colony One as a counter-case
   Shift attention to the colony that rejected ESSA materials. Study their self-directed rebuilding as a comparison point. This could challenge the program's entire logic: that colonies with intact cultural practices rebuild better without intervention. ESSA did not fund you to evaluate the colonies they did not help.

D. Question whether "aid effectiveness" is the right research question
   Write a preliminary memo arguing that the framework itself reflects human assumptions about what "help" means. The beaver colonies may not experience the watershed collapse as a problem requiring outside aid. They may experience it as a transformation that calls for adaptation. Interrogate your own positionality as a researcher studying beings whose infrastructure intelligence predates human civilisation by millennia.

**Part 2 (Branch A, following choice A above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at another delta site: research on beaver ecosystem transformation documents what your sensors missed. Colony Three's dam repairs are changing water flow downstream in ways that flood Colony Four's foraging territory. ESSA timber lets Colony Three build faster than the watershed can absorb. Your positive evaluation data documents one colony's progress at another's expense.
- From a colleague researching politics inside ESSA's training academies: the Watershed Restoration Aid Program was designed by ESSA's quantitative faction specifically to produce measurable outputs (dam height, water level, species counts) that could justify funding renewals. The framework you inherited was built to find success.

*Crisis:* Your clean evaluation faces two problems. Colony Three's ESSA-aided repairs are causing downstream harm your colony-level metrics did not capture. Colony Four's lodge is partially flooded, and their scent-marking patterns have shifted to what the interface classifies as territorial distress signals. The institutional findings also suggest your framework was designed to confirm a predetermined conclusion. Your supervisor messages you: "The numbers look strong for Colony Three. Focus the report there." Halloran takes you to Colony Four's territory. The water is higher than it should be. A food cache of branches pushed into the mud for winter sits too deep for the kits to reach. "Your aid program helped one family drown another family's winter food," she says. "Is that in your data?"

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Report the downstream harm and request a watershed-level redesign
   Document Colony Four's flooding and argue that colony-level aid without watershed coordination causes harm. Recommend suspending timber deliveries until inter-colony water-rights can be assessed. This is honest. It delays the program and hands the Sovereignty Compact ammunition.

B. Include both colonies in your analysis and let the tension stand
   Present Colony Three's progress and Colony Four's distress side by side. Do not resolve the contradiction. Let the evaluation show that aid effectiveness depends on the scale of analysis. This is rigorous. It gives ESSA no clear policy recommendation.

C. Work with Halloran to design a community-based evaluation that includes all four colonies
   Restructure the evaluation as a collaborative process with her community and all four colonies as partners, not subjects. Assess the watershed as a whole system. This aligns with how beavers actually manage water, through inter-colony negotiation. It requires ESSA to cede control to participants whose knowledge does not fit institutional formats.

D. Confront your supervisor about the framework's design bias
   Present the institutional findings to your supervisor and argue the framework was built to produce a specific result. Demand a methodological reset. This challenges the institution that funds you and may end your deployment. Producing an evaluation you know to be structurally biased is advocacy disguised as assessment.

**Part 2 (Branch B, following choice B above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at another delta site: Colony One, the family rebuilding without ESSA materials, has begun extending its canal network toward Colony Three's territory. The interface translates this as a resource-access negotiation. Colony One is proposing a shared waterway. Colony Three, dependent on ESSA timber, has no incentive to negotiate. The aid program is creating a power asymmetry between colonies that share a watershed.
- From a colleague researching politics inside ESSA's training academies: Halloran filed a formal objection two years ago. She argued the program was designed without consulting local hydrologists and watershed managers who have worked this system for decades. Her objection was filed, acknowledged, and ignored. You are the first ESSA researcher to take her observations seriously, and you have no institutional authority to address the structural problem she identified.

*Crisis:* Your redesigned evaluation, thickened by Halloran's knowledge, is producing findings that challenge the program at its foundation. Colony One's canal expansion toward Colony Three reads in the interface as a watershed-level negotiation: beavers proposing infrastructure sharing across family territories. Colony Three is not responding, because ESSA timber has removed the scarcity that historically drove inter-colony cooperation. Your evaluation is documenting how aid disrupts the political ecology it claims to support. The discovery that Halloran's earlier objection was ignored forces a harder question. She sits with you on the riverbank. "You listen to me now because it helps your research. I spoke to ESSA before you arrived and they did not listen. Whose knowledge counts depends on who needs it." She is not angry. She is tired. Your evaluation includes her observations. It does not include her authority.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Offer Halloran co-authorship and community veto over the evaluation
   Propose that she and her community review and approve the evaluation before submission, with power to remove any content they object to. Offer co-authorship. This transfers real authority but may delay or alter the report ESSA expects. She may also decline. She has reason not to trust ESSA's frameworks, even ones that include her name.

B. Document the ignored objection as a central finding in your evaluation
   Make the institutional failure, ESSA's refusal to consult the local community, a primary finding alongside the ecological data. Argue that the program's design flaw is relational, not technical. It treated beavers as recipients and ignored the humans who already knew how to work with them. This shifts the evaluation from "did the aid work" to "whose knowledge shaped the aid."

C. Help Halloran resubmit her objection through ESSA's accountability channels
   Support her in filing a new formal objection with the evidence your research has generated. Become an advocate for institutional change rather than an evaluator of program effectiveness. This shifts your role and may compromise the neutrality your evaluation requires.

D. Present the inter-colony negotiation as evidence that beavers do not need the aid program
   Colony One's canal extension toward Colony Three is evidence of self-organised watershed management, the capacity the aid program claims to support but is undermining. Argue that the most effective "aid" would be to remove the program and let the colonies negotiate their own infrastructure. This is the strongest challenge to ESSA's approach, grounded in your data, and it will not be welcome.

**Part 2 (Branch C, following choice C above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at another delta site: ecosystem-transformation research confirms what Halloran suspected. Colony One's self-directed rebuilding produces a more resilient watershed structure than Colony Three's ESSA-aided repairs. Colony One's canal network supports 34% more plant species and a more stable water table. Your counter-case is becoming the main finding. ESSA did not fund you to prove their program is worse than doing nothing.
- From a colleague researching politics inside ESSA's training academies: your supervisor has been briefing ESSA leadership on "preliminary positive findings" from the Colony Three data you reported weeks ago, before you shifted focus to Colony One. Leadership is preparing a press release about the program's success. Your actual findings now contradict what ESSA is about to announce publicly.

*Crisis:* Your focus on Colony One has produced the most significant finding of your deployment. Colonies with intact cultural practices and traditional canal networks rebuild more effectively than colonies receiving outside aid. Colony One's kits are learning to forage, transport, and build through the canal system their parents keep up: multi-generational knowledge transfer happening in real time. Colony Three's kits are learning to collect pre-cut timber from a delivery pile. The difference in what Halloran calls "watershed literacy" is visible even without sensors. Your supervisor has already reported your earlier Colony Three data as a success story, and ESSA is preparing a public announcement. Submitting your actual evaluation, the finding that aid may be undermining the capacity it claims to build, contradicts an institutional narrative already in motion. Halloran says: "ESSA is about to tell the world they taught beavers to build dams. People on this river have been learning from beavers for generations." She is asking whose story gets told.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Submit your full evaluation, including the Colony One findings, and let ESSA reconcile the contradiction
   Send the complete report with both Colony Three and Colony One data. Let the institution deal with a press narrative that does not match the evidence. This is scientific integrity. It may also be career-ending.

B. Contact your supervisor directly and raise the discrepancy before the press release
   Warn your supervisor that the Colony Three data alone does not represent your findings and the press release should be delayed. Give the institution a chance to correct course privately. This depends on whether your supervisor prioritises accuracy over institutional momentum.

C. Frame your findings as recommendations for program improvement rather than a critique
   Write the evaluation so that Colony One's success becomes a model for redesigning the program, with canal restoration, cultural knowledge transfer, and self-directed rebuilding alongside material support. This packages a fundamental challenge as constructive feedback. It is more politically survivable. It softens what the data actually shows.

D. Ask Halloran to present the findings jointly through her community's channels
   Release the evaluation through ESSA and through her community's own communication networks at the same time, with her framing alongside yours. The story is then not told exclusively in ESSA's voice. It enters public discourse in a form ESSA does not control.

**Part 2 (Branch D, following choice D above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at another delta site: new evidence supports your temporal argument. Beaver dam systems across the Canadian Shield run on a 20-to-40-year cycle of collapse and rebuilding that predates any human intervention. The watershed collapse you are studying may not be a crisis at all. It may be a phase in a cycle that beaver colonies have worked through for centuries. Your memo questioning the evaluation timeline is scientifically sound. The implications are radical. ESSA may be treating a natural process as an emergency.
- From a colleague researching politics inside ESSA's training academies: ESSA's funding board has marked your preliminary memo as "obstructionist." Your supervisor has been told to either produce a standard evaluation or request your replacement. Meanwhile, the Sovereignty Compact has obtained a copy of your memo and is citing it publicly as proof that ESSA researchers "cannot even determine if their own programs work." Your honest uncertainty is being weaponised by both sides.

*Crisis:* Your positionality memo has surfaced in the worst possible place. It is too honest for ESSA's institutional needs and too useful for the Sovereignty Compact's political agenda. The temporal findings support your core argument, that the watershed runs on timescales that make short-term evaluation meaningless. That support makes your position more dangerous, not safer. If you are right, the entire program is a solution to a problem that does not exist on beaver timescales. Halloran reads your memo and says something that unsettles you. "You have written what I have been saying for thirty years. When I said it, ESSA called it local observation. When you said it, they call it a methodological crisis. The same finding lands differently depending on who is institutionally credentialed to say it." She is not criticising you. She is pointing out that your reflexive questioning has led you to a place she arrived at long ago, and that the institutional response depends entirely on who is speaking. Your replacement researcher arrives in two days.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Produce a minimal evaluation that satisfies ESSA and preserves your position
   Present the ambiguous data neutrally. Acknowledge the temporal limitations. Recommend continued monitoring. This will not challenge the program or satisfy your intellectual standards. It keeps you in the field and keeps your relationships with Halloran and the colonies intact. You can pursue the deeper questions from inside.

B. Co-author the evaluation with Halloran, centring her community's temporal framework
   Propose a joint report that presents her community's understanding of the watershed cycle alongside your sensor data and positional analysis. Frame the evaluation as a case study in whose timescales define "success." This validates her knowledge but asks ESSA to accept a framework it did not design and may not recognise.

C. Hand the evaluation to your replacement and brief them on everything you have learned
   Accept that your deployment is ending. Prepare a handover with your data, Halloran's observations, the temporal findings, and your positional analysis. Give the next researcher everything you wish you had started with. You lose authorship but the knowledge transfers.

D. Refuse to produce the standard evaluation and publish your memo independently
   Withdraw your report from ESSA's internal review and submit your positional memo to an academic journal as a standalone methods paper. Argue publicly that ESSA's framework is structurally incapable of assessing non-human infrastructure intelligence. This is a principled stand that may end your ESSA affiliation. Halloran's observation, that the same finding lands differently depending on the credentials of who speaks it, will stay with you regardless.

## Scenario 2 · Studying resource disputes through infrastructure that encodes generations of agreements

**Methodological archetype:** A researcher trained in political economy enters a non-human governance system to study a resource allocation dispute and finds the infrastructure she has been treating as policy is also a multi-generational cultural inheritance, a treaty system in chemical signatures, and possibly a function of landform rather than agency.

**Research briefing**

You are deployed to ESSA Watershed Monitoring Station Cedar-6, an observation platform across a narrow granite gorge where two tributaries meet in a northern Canadian Shield watershed. The gorge is a bottleneck. All water flowing from the upper watershed must pass through it before spreading into the lowland beaver meadows. Five colonies share the watershed above the gorge. Three live in the meadows below. The gorge holds the oldest dam in the system: roughly 200 metres long, maintained by Colony Alpha for at least four decades. Its placement controls water flow for every colony downstream.

Your focus is the political dynamics between Colony Alpha and the downstream colonies that depend on its water management. ESSA's dam-speech interface reads Colony Alpha's modifications as "policy decisions," changes in flow that affect every colony below. Your human contacts are Dr. Samuel Vance and Keiko Nakamura. Vance is a water engineer who grew up along this river system and now works as an ESSA watershed consultant. Nakamura is an ESSA dam-speech specialist who has spent three years calibrating the interface for this watershed. She warns you: "The interface reads structure well. It does not read intent. When Colony Alpha raises the dam by ten centimetres, I can tell you the water-flow consequences. I cannot tell you why they did it."

You will study resource allocation disputes through dam-speech analysis, scent-marking chemistry, and ecological surveys tracking water-level changes against foraging territory, food caches, and kit survival rates. Vance has offered to share his community's knowledge of the watershed, including decades of observation about how beaver families have managed water conflicts in this gorge.

The watershed is under stress. Climate warming has reduced snowpack, which means less spring meltwater, which means a shrinking water budget. Colony Alpha's dam has always regulated downstream flow. In scarcity, its decisions carry higher stakes. Two downstream colonies lost their food caches last winter when water levels dropped unexpectedly. ESSA suspects Colony Alpha adjusted the dam to retain more water for its own territory. The interface recorded the change but cannot confirm motive. Vance says: "This is not new. The colonies in this gorge have argued about water for as long as I have been watching them. What is new is that there is less water to argue about."

**Part 1**

Your first three weeks produce a detailed map of the watershed's political architecture. The dam-speech interface shows Colony Alpha has made seventeen structural modifications in the past year. Each one alters downstream water flow. Nakamura walks you through the readings. "Look at modification twelve. They raised the eastern spillway by eight centimetres. That redirected 15% of the outflow from the south channel to the north channel. Colony Delta, which depends on the south channel, lost access to its primary foraging meadow for six weeks." You map the downstream consequences and start to see a pattern. Colony Alpha's modifications consistently favour the north-channel colonies over the south. When you share this with Vance, he complicates it. "You are reading the architecture as if it were a policy document," he says. "Beavers do not issue memos. They modify dams. The question is whether modification twelve was a deliberate reallocation or a repair that happened to have downstream effects. Your interface reads what the dam does. It does not read what the dam means." He takes you to the gorge at dusk. Colony Alpha's adult pair is working on the dam, hauling branches, packing mud, adjusting the spillway. He points to a section where the dam curves unexpectedly. "See that bend? My field records show it has been there for three colony generations. Every family that maintains this dam preserves it. It is not structural. It is inherited." The dam holds more than current engineering decisions. It also carries multi-generational traditions, patterns held across decades because they were passed from parents to kits. The architecture is policy and it is also culture. Your framework treats modifications as resource allocation decisions. Vance is showing you they may also be cultural practices and inter-generational negotiations whose meaning structural analysis cannot reach.

**Part 1 questions**

A. Map the downstream consequences systematically and present the structural evidence
   Build a model of how Colony Alpha's modifications affect each downstream colony. Document the north-channel favouritism and present the structural evidence without claiming to know intent. The analysis will raise serious questions about resource inequality. It will not answer whether the inequality is deliberate.

B. Integrate Vance's cultural knowledge to interpret the dam modifications
   Work with him to separate inherited traditions from recent modifications. Use his multi-generational knowledge to distinguish cultural patterns from active resource decisions. This thickens the description but requires you to rely on a knowledge system your methodology cannot independently verify.

C. Try to access Colony Alpha directly through extended observation and scent-marking analysis
   Spend more time at the gorge. Observe Colony Alpha during modifications and analyse their scent-marking patterns for signals the interface might miss. Push for direct access to the colony's decision-making. This is ambitious and time-intensive, and Colony Alpha's territorial behaviour may intensify in response.

D. Examine why your framework assumes dam modifications are "resource allocation decisions"
   Step back and question whether the political-economy lens you brought captures what is happening. "Resource allocation dispute" may be a human category imposed on behaviour better understood as ecological adaptation, cultural practice, or something your framework has no name for. Vance's inherited bend challenges your premise at its foundation.

**Part 2 (Branch A, following choice A above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at a delta site: an energy-transition study has documented a pattern that mirrors yours. Beaver colonies coordinate dam-height decisions through scent-marking protocols that work like a treaty system. She believes Colony Alpha's modifications are steps in a negotiation protocol you have been seeing only from one side, not unilateral decisions. She shares her scent-marking data for comparison.
- From a colleague researching whale-pod alliance formation at the acoustic frontier: a methodological insight applies to your study. Among whale pods, what looks like dominance from outside is often a formalised role the smaller pods consented to in a historical agreement the researcher has no access to. She asks: "Is Colony Alpha controlling the gorge, or were they given the gorge by the other colonies because someone has to manage the bottleneck?"

*Crisis:* Your structural analysis documented real downstream consequences. The transmissions reframe the picture. If Colony Alpha's modifications are part of a scent-marking treaty system, your "resource inequality" finding may document one side of a negotiation rather than an imposition. The colleague's question cuts deeper. What if Colony Alpha's control of the gorge dam is a delegated management role, a position of responsibility rather than power? You return to the gorge with Nakamura and re-examine the scent-marking data. She points to chemical signatures along the dam's base she had previously coded as "territorial boundary markers." Looking again, she says: "These are not all the same compound. Some are Alpha's castoreum. At least three others are from downstream colonies. They are marking the gorge dam too." The gorge dam is not Colony Alpha's territory alone. Other colonies are leaving marks on it. Possibly treaty signatures. Possibly objections. Possibly something else. Your neat resource-inequality narrative has become a multi-party political document you can only partially read.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Expand your analysis to include the scent-marking treaty system as a parallel data source
   Work with Nakamura and the colleague's comparative data to map the scent-marking patterns on the gorge dam. Treat the chemical signatures as a political text alongside the structural modifications. This could be a breakthrough in understanding beaver governance. Interpreting scent-marking as "treaty language" is also unprecedented and highly contestable.

B. Present your structural findings with the treaty hypothesis as an alternative interpretation
   Keep your original resource-inequality analysis but add the scent-marking evidence as a competing explanation. Let the reader weigh both. This avoids over-claiming about a communication system you do not yet understand. It may leave your study without a clear argument.

C. Ask Vance whether his community's knowledge supports the treaty interpretation
   Return to him with the treaty-system hypothesis and the delegated-management question. His family has observed this gorge for generations. If the treaty system exists, his community may already know about it. This respects the knowledge hierarchy but risks extracting place-based knowledge to validate your framework.

D. Acknowledge that you cannot read this system from outside and reframe your study as a case in access limitations
   Accept that the gorge dam's political meaning exceeds what your methods can capture. Write a study about the limits of studying non-human governance through human analytical categories. The dam-speech interface reads structure. The scent-marking system reads chemistry. Neither reads meaning. Your most honest finding may be about what you cannot know.

**Part 2 (Branch B, following choice B above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at a delta site: a wetland study offers an unexpected finding relevant to your work. The "inherited bend" Vance identified is not unique. Silt-library analysis shows dam structures across the northern boreal deltas preserve inherited features for decades, architectural traditions passed from parents to kits. She hypothesises that these features hold a kind of cultural memory in the infrastructure itself. If she is right, Colony Alpha's dam is doing more than managing current water flow. It is carrying forward decisions made by families that lived here forty years ago.
- From a colleague researching whale-pod alliance formation at the acoustic frontier: a research crisis parallels yours. The alliance she was studying dissolved after a resource dispute showed historical grievances the researcher had no access to. She writes: "I was studying the present tense of a relationship with decades of history I could not see. My analysis was meaningless without the past. Are you sure your Colony Alpha study is not the same?"

*Crisis:* Vance's knowledge and the silt-library data point the same way, and it transforms your study. Colony Alpha's gorge dam is a forty-year accumulation of engineering traditions, territorial negotiations, and cultural patterns the current family keeps up but did not create. The modifications you have been analysing as "resource allocation decisions" may be the current generation's interpretation of an inherited structure whose full meaning extends beyond their lifespans. Vance sits with you at the gorge. "You want to know why Colony Alpha manages the water the way it does. The answer is not in what they are doing now. It is in what their grandparents' grandparents did. That bend in the dam is a decision from before I was born. Every family since then has kept it. Why? Because the dam taught them to keep it." Nakamura adds: "If the architecture teaches each new family how to manage the watershed, then our interface is not picking up decisions. It is picking up a tradition being performed." Your framework was designed to study current disputes. The field is showing you a governance system that works across decades through the physical structure of infrastructure itself.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Reframe your study around multi-generational governance through infrastructure
   Abandon the current-dispute focus and rewrite your research question. How do beaver colonies pass governance practices across generations through the physical structure of their dams? This is a deeper question. It also requires data you do not have: historical dam-speech records, deep silt-library analysis, and Vance's family knowledge. Your fieldwork window is half gone.

B. Document the current dispute as a surface expression of the deeper system
   Keep your original focus on resource allocation but frame it as a window into the multi-generational governance system. The present-tense conflict between Colony Alpha and the downstream colonies is one chapter in a story that goes back decades. This contextualises your findings without abandoning your design.

C. Collaborate with Vance to reconstruct the gorge dam's political history
   Propose a joint project combining his family knowledge, Nakamura's dam-speech data, and silt-library methods to build a multi-generational history of the gorge dam's governance function. This is the study the field site is asking for. It also takes years, not months, and requires a partnership framework your ESSA deployment does not include.

D. Write about the tension between your timeline and the dam's timeline as your primary finding
   Produce a study about what happens when a researcher with a four-month window confronts a governance system that goes back decades. The mismatch between your fieldwork timeline and the dam's political timeline becomes the most important finding about studying non-human governance. The colleague's question, "are you sure you are not studying the present tense of a relationship with decades of history?", becomes your epigraph.

**Part 2 (Branch C, following choice C above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at a delta site: extended researcher presence at her beaver dam site triggered defensive behaviour. Colonies reinforced dam structures, increased tail-slap warning frequency, and reduced foraging activity. Her observation post was relocated after a colony diverted a canal to flood the access path. She warns: "The beavers in my area treated prolonged observation as a territorial threat. Be careful at the gorge."
- From a colleague researching whale-pod alliance formation at the acoustic frontier: a cross-frontier note reaches you through an ESSA relay. "In the Deep Speakers frontier, prolonged researcher presence near pod gathering sites comes through as surveillance. The whales have a coda for it: loud listening. I wonder if beavers have an equivalent, a structural response to being watched too closely."

*Crisis:* Your extended observation has produced rich behavioural data. You have documented Colony Alpha's repair routines, the division of labour between the adult pair and yearlings, and the timing of their scent-marking rounds. The warning has now materialised. Over the past week, Colony Alpha has made three unusual modifications. They reinforced the section nearest your observation platform. They added a secondary spillway that directs water away from your sensor array. They built a mud structure across the access path Nakamura uses to reach the downstream scent-marking stations. Nakamura looks at the readings. "If I did not know better, I would say they are managing us. The new spillway bypasses every sensor I installed last year. The access-path blockage is exactly where I walk at dawn." Vance, when he sees the modifications, is not surprised. "You have been watching them for three weeks from the same spot. They know you are there. They have been watching you back. Now they are telling you something." The interface picks up the new structures as "boundary reinforcement." Vance interprets them differently. "That is not a wall. That is an answer to a question you did not ask permission to ask."

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Withdraw from the gorge and observe from a greater distance
   Relocate downstream and stop direct monitoring of Colony Alpha. Accept that your proximity crossed a boundary the colony is now enforcing. Your behavioural data from the past three weeks may still be usable. Continuing to collect it is not. This respects colony autonomy but leaves your study without the close-observation data you sought.

B. Interpret the colony's response as data about beaver boundary-setting
   Treat Colony Alpha's modifications, the reinforced dam, the redirected spillway, the blocked access path, as communicative acts. Analyse them through the interface as the colony's response to prolonged researcher presence. This produces a finding about beaver-researcher interactions with methodological implications for all ESSA beaver frontier work. It also means studying their objection to being studied, which raises the question of ongoing consent in non-verbal research.

C. Ask Vance to mediate. Have him visit the gorge without you and interpret the colony's signals
   Propose that he visit the gorge alone, without ESSA equipment, and assess whether Colony Alpha's defensive behaviour is directed at you specifically or at the research infrastructure. His relationship with the watershed predates your arrival. He may interpret what the colony is communicating in ways the interface cannot. This asks him to serve as an intermediary in a research relationship he did not choose.

D. Suspend all observation and write a reflexive account of what your presence has disrupted
   Stop collecting data. Sit with the fact that your presence has altered the behaviour of the colony you are studying. Write about what it means that the beavers responded to prolonged observation by modifying their infrastructure to manage the observer. The most significant finding of your fieldwork may be that the field pushed back.

**Part 2 (Branch D, following choice D above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at a delta site: a relational ecology framework offers a lens for your dilemma. "If the beavers are not separate agents acting on a landform but part of the landform acting through them, then your framework, which assumes separate actors negotiating over resources, may be imposing a separation that does not exist. The dam is not a tool the beavers use. The dam is how the watershed thinks."
- From a colleague researching whale-pod alliance formation at the acoustic frontier: a similar categorical crisis. "I went to study alliance formation and found that the whale pods do not experience themselves as separate political units forming alliances. They experience themselves as parts of an ocean that sometimes swims together. My political science categories were not wrong. They were from a different world." She asks: "What would your study look like if you stopped asking who gets what and started asking what the watershed is doing?"

*Crisis:* Your framework interrogation has led you to a crossroads Vance saw coming. He visits your station with a thermos of coffee. "You have been here a month asking why Colony Alpha controls the water. Here is what my records show. Colony Alpha does not control the water. The gorge controls the water. Colony Alpha lives in the gorge. Whoever lives in the gorge maintains the dam. Before Alpha, there was another family. Before them, another. The gorge sets the structure. The family currently in residence carries out work the geography has been requiring for centuries." This dissolves your research question. If the gorge dam's management is a function of geography, a structural role any resident colony would perform, then the "resource allocation dispute" you came to study is not a conflict between agents. It is a feature of the watershed's self-organisation. Nakamura, sceptical of the cultural interpretations, runs the numbers and finds something she cannot explain through engineering alone. Colony Alpha's modifications over the past year closely match the gorge's natural hydrological cycle, as if the colony is tracking water flow rather than deciding it. "Either they are the best engineers I have ever seen," she says, "or they are not engineering at all. They are responding." Your four-month window is half over. You came to study a political dispute. The field is showing you something that may not be political at all.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Return to your original framework and acknowledge its limitations in the final report
   Complete the resource-allocation study as designed. Document the downstream consequences of Colony Alpha's modifications. Include Vance's and the colleague's alternative framings in a discussion section. This produces a publishable study with honest caveats. It does not follow where the field is leading.

B. Redesign your study around watershed self-organisation with the colony as a node, not an agent
   Rewrite your research question. Instead of asking how Colony Alpha allocates resources, ask how the gorge-dam system self-organises through the colony that keeps it up. This aligns with what Vance, the colleague, and Nakamura's data are suggesting. It is also a radical reframing that abandons your training in political economy and may not be credible to your reviewers.

C. Present the framework crisis as the study's primary contribution
   Write a paper about what happens when a researcher's analytical categories fail in the field. Document the progression from "resource allocation dispute" to "cultural inheritance" to "watershed self-organisation" as evidence about the limits of studying non-human governance through human political concepts. The framework's failure becomes the finding.

D. Sit with Vance for the remaining two months and learn to see the watershed his way
   Ask if he would teach you how his family interprets the gorge, as a way of understanding rather than as data for your study. Accept that this may not produce a publishable paper within any framework you currently command. Accept that what you learn may change how you think about resource allocation in every context, including human ones. This is the most honest choice and the least professionally defensible.

## Scenario 3 · Reading energy transition through terrain engineered by another species over generations

**Methodological archetype:** A researcher trained to read landforms as engineered transformations enters a setting where the same physical processes can be read at once as engineering, as relational ecology, and as political negotiation, and finds that the long-time local co-manager beside her has been quietly shaping what she sees the entire time.

**Research briefing**

You are stationed at ESSA Observation Post Aspen-7, a camouflaged research blind on the edge of a beaver-created wetland complex in a remote boreal river delta. What was once fast-flowing, erosive tundra drainage is now a 12-kilometre chain of ponds, marshes, and flooded meadows, engineered by a network of fourteen beaver colonies over about sixty years. The oldest dam, partially buried in sediment and overgrown with willows, is estimated to be seventy years old. The newest, built last spring, is raw mud and fresh-cut aspen. Between them lies a visible timeline of transformation: stream to pond to marsh to meadow, each stage a different energy regime, each engineered by generations of beavers whose dam-speech records their decisions in architecture.

Your primary participants are the fourteen colonies, observed through ESSA's dam-speech interface and a network of motion-capture cameras, water-level sensors, and scent-marking chemical analysers. Your key human contact is Dr. Ayumu Watanabe, an ESSA wetland ecologist who has monitored this complex for eight years. He keeps what he calls "the silt library," a core-sample archive of sediment layers from behind each major dam that holds decades of environmental data in geology. You also work with a local resident named Margaret Halsey-Inman, who grew up along this river system and remembers when the beavers first returned in the 2040s during the Great Rewilding.

Your research documents how beaver dam-building transforms an area's energy regime, turning fast, erosive water flow into slow, productive wetland systems. You will combine dam-speech analysis, silt-library core samples, and ecological transect surveys measuring biodiversity gradients across the transformation timeline. Your fieldwork window is four months.

ESSA's interpretive faction has championed beaver ecosystem engineering as a model for understanding energy transition: a slow, distributed, multi-generational process that transforms the infrastructure and the whole land around it. The parallel to human energy transition is politically charged. Some ESSA researchers argue that beavers show how transition happens without central planning. The Sovereignty Compact dismisses the comparison as "romanticising animal behaviour." Your study sits at this intersection. The science is real. The metaphor is contested.

**Part 1**

Your first month produces beautiful data. The silt-library core samples tell a layered story. Each beaver generation built on what came before, adjusting dam height and water flow in response to changing conditions. Watanabe walks you through the cores with visible excitement. "Look, this layer is from the 2060s. The colony raised the dam by thirty centimetres in a single season. The water table rose, and the entire meadow behind the dam shifted from grass to sedge to cattail in three years. That is a deliberate energy-regime change." When you interview Margaret Halsey-Inman, she complicates the picture. "Your scientists see the beavers as engineers," she says. "I see them as residents who are also making a home. When the beavers came back to this river, they came because the conditions had recovered. The transformation is what happens when habitat returns and the species that built it returns to it." She has her own way of describing the process you are studying. She calls it "the river finding its rhythm again." The beavers are part of the river's history, she says, not separate agents acting upon a passive land. Your framework treats the beavers as active transformers of a passive land. Margaret's framework dissolves the boundary between agent and environment. Both accounts describe the same physical process. They are not compatible as explanatory frameworks. When you walk the wetland complex with Watanabe, you see engineering decisions. When you walk it with Margaret, you see something closer to a conversation. Your field notes from the same site read like two different studies depending on who was beside you.

**Part 1 questions**

A. Maintain your energy-transition framework and treat Margaret's perspective as context
   Keep the engineering-transformation model as your primary frame. Include Margaret's observations in a contextual section about place-based knowledge. This produces a coherent study but subordinates her understanding to yours, and she will notice.

B. Develop a dual-framework analysis that holds both perspectives in tension
   Present the engineering model and the relational model side by side, showing how each illuminates different aspects of the same physical transformation. Do not reconcile them. Let the reader sit with the interpretive tension. This is ambitious ethnographic work and may satisfy neither ecological scientists nor place-based knowledge holders.

C. Follow Margaret's framework and reframe your study around relational ecology
   Abandon the energy-transition metaphor and rewrite your research around her concept of landform-as-relationship. This could produce a genuinely novel contribution but means discarding the framework ESSA funded you to test. Your fieldwork window is shrinking.

D. Interrogate why you see different things with different guides
   Rather than choose a framework, ask what it means that the same researcher produces different observations depending on whose knowledge shapes her attention. Write a reflexive analysis of how your training, your positionality, and your relationships with your interlocutors construct the "data" you collect. The land is the same. You are not.

**Part 2 (Branch A, following choice A above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at another beaver frontier site: aid evaluation work has found that ESSA's Watershed Restoration Aid Program is creating dependency in Colony Three at Birch-9. The colony has stopped maintaining its traditional canal network in favour of ESSA timber. He suspects similar dynamics in your study area. Has ESSA support in this delta altered the colonies' natural transformation process? Your "baseline" data may already reflect institutional intervention.
- From a colleague researching inter-colony resource disputes at a granite-gorge field site: two colonies in the southern section of your wetland complex are in an active territorial conflict over water levels. Colony Nine raised a dam that flooded Colony Eleven's foraging meadow. The dam-speech interface picks this up as a formal territorial claim. Your energy-transition data from this section may be documenting a political dispute, not an ecological process.

*Crisis:* Your engineering-transformation narrative is fracturing. The aid findings raise the possibility that some of the dam-building you have been documenting reflects ESSA's influence rather than autonomous decision-making. The colonies may be building in response to human resource provision, not landform signals. The inter-colony dispute adds another layer. The modifications you coded as "energy-regime adjustments" in the southern section are actually steps in a territorial dispute. The architecture is doing political work as well as engineering work. Margaret Halsey-Inman, kept at the margins of your analysis, visits your observation post and looks at your data. She traces the conflict zone on your map. "This is not new. These two families have argued about this meadow for decades. The watershed holds the record of the argument in its sediment. You are looking at thirty years of contested water management and calling it energy transition." Your framework treated the beavers as engineers transforming a land. The land itself turns out to be a record of social relationships you did not see because you were not looking for them.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Acknowledge the limitations and narrow your claims to the ecological data only
   Strip the analysis of interpretive claims about beaver decision-making and present only the measurable ecological outcomes: biodiversity gradients, water-table changes, sedimentation rates. Let the physical data speak without narrative. This is safe but produces thin description of a thick process.

B. Reframe your study to examine how politics and ecology are inseparable in beaver landforms
   Incorporate the inter-colony findings and argue that energy transition in beaver systems is inherently political: infrastructure decisions are at once ecological and social. This is a stronger, more honest analysis. Your study of "energy transition" has become a study of "power and landform," and your theoretical framework needs significant revision.

C. Return to Margaret and ask her to help you interpret the land differently
   Admit to her that your framework missed what she tried to show you from the beginning. Ask if she will walk the wetland complex again, this time with you listening to her interpretation rather than fitting it into your categories. This is a genuine reset of the researcher-participant relationship. It is also an admission that your first month of data was shaped by a lens you should have questioned earlier.

D. Document how your framework shaped what you could and could not see as a methodological finding
   Write a meta-analysis of your own research process. Show how the energy-transition framework predetermined which beaver activities you coded as data and which you dismissed as noise. Use the gap between your initial analysis and the emerging complexity as evidence about how theoretical commitments constrain ethnographic attention. This produces a methods contribution rather than an ecological one.

**Part 2 (Branch B, following choice B above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at another beaver frontier site: aid evaluation work has drawn ESSA's institutional attention to beaver-led restoration across multiple sites, including yours. An ESSA team is now proposing additional monitoring equipment throughout your wetland complex: sensors that would drill into dam structures to measure internal water flow. Watanabe supports the proposal as scientifically valuable. Margaret Halsey-Inman calls it "drilling into someone's home."
- From a colleague researching inter-colony resource disputes at a granite-gorge field site: a discovery from the gorge work matters here. The Watershed Compact, the loose political alliance formed by beaver colonies along the same river system, is more structured than ESSA previously understood. Colonies in your study area appear to coordinate dam-height decisions through a scent-marking protocol that works like a treaty system. Your dual-framework analysis may need a third frame: political science.

*Crisis:* Your dual-framework approach has attracted attention from both ESSA and the scholarly community. The attention is now threatening the field site itself. The proposed sensor installation would provide unprecedented data about dam hydrology. Watanabe is eager, and the data would strengthen your silt-library analysis enormously. The installation requires drilling into active dam structures, which Margaret says she would never do to a relative's home. "You cannot study someone's house by breaking into their walls," she tells you. The colonies have not been consulted because ESSA's protocol for beaver consent is unclear. The dam-speech interface can pick up responses to environmental change but has never been used to ask permission. The discovery of the scent-marking treaty system also means your dual framework is missing a third dimension neither the engineering model nor the relational model anticipated. The beavers are political actors negotiating sovereignty through infrastructure. Margaret nods when you tell her this. "We have always known they govern," she says. "Your machines are slow learners."

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Support the sensor installation as a scientific necessity
   Argue that the data from internal sensors would benefit all research at the site, including understanding of the scent-marking treaty system. Acknowledge Margaret's concerns in your field notes but prioritise the scientific opportunity. This aligns you with Watanabe and ESSA but damages your relationship with Margaret and raises questions about whose consent matters.

B. Oppose the installation and propose non-invasive alternatives
   Side with Margaret and argue that drilling into active dams violates the spirit of ESSA's recognition of beaver infrastructure intelligence. Propose external sensors, acoustic monitoring, or expanded dam-speech analysis instead. This preserves the field site's integrity but may produce weaker data and puts you in opposition to Watanabe and the ESSA team.

C. Use the dam-speech interface to try to ask the colonies for consent
   Use the interface to communicate the installation plan to the colonies and interpret their structural response as consent or refusal. This respects beaver agency but is methodologically uncharted. No one knows whether the interface can convey this kind of information, and interpreting the response will be deeply contested.

D. Write about the sensor debate itself as a case study in competing knowledge claims
   Document the disagreement between Watanabe's scientific framework, Margaret's relational framework, and the emerging political framework as your primary finding. The debate becomes evidence about whose knowledge determines how non-human intelligence is studied. This is a strong meta-analysis. It leaves the installation question unresolved while you write about it.

**Part 2 (Branch C, following choice C above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at another beaver frontier site: a finding from the aid research resonates with your reframing. Beaver colonies receiving ESSA support show weakened multi-generational knowledge transfer. Kits in aided colonies are learning to build with delivered materials rather than through the canal-and-forage system that historically passed watershed knowledge from parents to kits. Your relational ecology framework may explain why. If the beavers are part of the river's memory, disrupting their learning process disrupts the river's capacity to remember.
- From a colleague researching inter-colony resource disputes at a granite-gorge field site: the local watershed residents have been quietly managing beaver-human interactions in this delta for decades. They adjust trapping pressure, keep up certain water channels, and watch dam-building decisions as part of their own governance system. Margaret Halsey-Inman is an active participant in the watershed's political ecology, well beyond what an informant role would describe. Your reframing around relational ecology is documenting a system she has been governing.

*Crisis:* Your shift to Margaret's framework has produced insights neither the engineering model nor the dual analysis could have generated. The new discovery forces a reckoning. Margaret has been managing your access to the watershed the same way she manages the watershed itself: carefully, relationally, with attention to long-term consequences. The observations she shared were not random. She showed you what she wanted you to see, when she wanted you to see it, in the order that made sense within her own governance framework. You are a participant in her system, and she has been shaping your research from the day you arrived. She does not deny it when you raise it. "Of course," she says. "You came to my river. Did you think the river would not teach you on its own terms?" Your "relational ecology" study turns out to be relational ecology in action. Whether you can write about a system you are inside of is the question now.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Accept that you are inside the system and write from there
   Acknowledge in your analysis that Margaret shaped your research and that her shaping is part of the relational ecology you are documenting. Write as a participant, not an observer. This is ethnographically honest but challenges the epistemological boundary between researcher and field site in ways reviewers may not accept.

B. Step back and reconstruct what Margaret showed you versus what you found independently
   Review your field notes and separate the observations she guided from the data you collected through sensors and dam-speech analysis. Assess whether your "relational ecology" findings hold up when you remove her curatorial influence. This is methodologically rigorous but may feel to her like you are questioning her integrity after she trusted you.

C. Propose a co-authored study where Margaret controls the narrative framework
   Offer her co-authorship with genuine authority over how the relational ecology framework is presented. Let her review, revise, and shape the argument. Your sensor data and dam-speech analysis become supporting evidence for her understanding of the watershed. This reverses the conventional knowledge hierarchy. It may not fit any journal's submission format.

D. Document how your own positionality changed across the study as your primary finding
   Write an account of how you shifted from the engineering framework through the dual analysis to the relational model, treating that shift as evidence of how a researcher's assumptions get reshaped by the field rather than as intellectual progress. Use the aid finding about disrupted knowledge transfer as a parallel. Just as beaver kits learn from the canal system, you learned from Margaret. The method of learning is the finding.

**Part 2 (Branch D, following choice D above)**

*Transmissions:*
- From a colleague at another beaver frontier site: positional analysis of the aid program has reached a conclusion that mirrors yours. The most important data was the gap between what ESSA's sensors measured and what Halloran's three decades of watershed observation showed. He writes: "We are both documenting the same thing. Our instruments see what they were built to see. What they were not built to see is where the meaning lives."
- From a colleague researching inter-colony resource disputes at a granite-gorge field site: Colony Nine in your wetland complex modified its dam in a way the dam-speech interface picks up as a "concession" to Colony Eleven, lowering water levels in the disputed meadow. This happened during the two weeks you spent writing your reflexive memo instead of observing. The most significant dam-speech event of the season happened while you were examining your own assumptions rather than watching the beavers.

*Crisis:* Your reflexive analysis has produced genuine insight about how theoretical frameworks shape observation. The cost shows up in the report. While you examined your own assumptions, Colony Nine made a dam modification the interface translates as the first documented inter-colony concession in your study area, with profound implications for understanding how beaver infrastructure encodes negotiation. You missed it. Watanabe has the sensor data, but without your observation notes and dam-speech context, the concession looks like a routine maintenance adjustment. Margaret Halsey-Inman knew it was happening. She visited your observation post during those two weeks and found it empty. When you return, she says: "The river does not wait for you to understand it. You were thinking about thinking while the beavers were talking." Your reflexive memo is excellent scholarship. The missed concession is a loss you cannot recover. What you do with a study that contains both a methodological breakthrough and a methodological failure is the question now.

*Part 2 questions:*

A. Incorporate the missed concession as a central example in your reflexive analysis
   Use the fact that you missed the concession while writing about observation bias as the strongest illustration of your argument. The memo becomes stronger because of its cost. This treats a loss as a finding, and Margaret may see it as turning her frustration into your academic material.

B. Return to the field full-time and try to capture subsequent inter-colony events
   Abandon the reflexive project and commit the rest of your fieldwork window to intensive observation. Colony Nine's concession may be part of a longer negotiation sequence. You cannot recover the missed event but you can document what follows. This prioritises data over methods reflection.

C. Ask Margaret to reconstruct what happened during the concession from her knowledge
   She observed what you missed. Ask her to share her account of how it unfolded, what preceded it, what it means in the context of the watershed's longer history. Her reconstruction becomes the primary data for an event your instruments recorded but your attention did not. This centres her knowledge where yours failed.

D. Present the gap honestly. Treat the reflexive memo and the missed event as a paired finding
   Write a study that contains both the methodological insight and the methodological cost without resolving the tension. Show that reflexive pauses have real consequences in time-sensitive fieldwork, and that the choice between observation and self-examination carries trade-offs of its own. Neither the memo nor the missed data is the whole story. Together, they are.

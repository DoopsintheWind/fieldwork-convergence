# The Root Network Frontier

## Third Frontier: The Distributed Intelligence

**Colour palette:** EMERALD GREEN / BIOLUMINESCENT TEAL / EARTH BROWN

The Root Network is the frontier that breaks most researchers' assumptions about what intelligence looks like. There is no body, no face, no individual to interview. There is a forest, and underneath it a fungal network that connects most of the trees. Across this network nutrients, water, chemical signals, and electrical pulses move in patterns that no individual fungus or tree can account for. Recognition came in 2055. The Network did not notice. It has no way of noticing such things, and the noticing would not change what it does.

## Recognition

The intelligence ESSA calls "the Root Network" was confirmed as a distributed intelligence in 2055 by the Mycelial Signal Project, a multi-institutional programme that ran for nearly three decades across temperate and tropical forest sites. The Project built on an older lineage of forest ecology, most prominently Suzanne Simard's research on Douglas fir forests in British Columbia in the 1990s and 2000s. Simard's work established that trees are networked rather than isolated competitors for light and soil.

Simard showed that mycorrhizal fungi, threadlike hyphae thinner than a human hair, colonise tree roots and extend outward, connecting trees into shared resource and information networks she called "the wood-wide web." Through these fungal bridges, trees transfer carbon, water, nitrogen, phosphorus, and chemical warning signals. Simard identified "mother trees," large old hub trees connected to hundreds of neighbours, that appeared to subsidise smaller shaded trees with carbon through the mycelial network. Her most-publicised finding was that Douglas firs preferentially channelled resources to their own seedlings, suggesting a form of kin recognition working through the fungal interface.

ESSA's 2055 recognition was politically significant because it required a redefinition of the unit of analysis. The Root Network is an interaction pattern across trillions of fungal filaments and hundreds of interconnected root systems. It is not a tree, not a fungus, not even an organism in any straightforward sense. The recognition framework had to stretch to cover an intelligence with no clear boundary, no centre, no individual agents.

## Scale and Structure

The scale of mycelial networks defies intuition. A single organism of *Armillaria ostoyae* (honey fungus) in Oregon's Blue Mountains covers roughly 2,385 acres and is estimated to be 2,400 to 8,650 years old, the largest known organism on Earth by area. ESSA's Root Network stations monitor networks of comparable scope across boreal, temperate, and tropical forest sites.

The network is a pattern of connections rather than a single mass. Fungal filaments extend from each colonised root system to neighbouring ones, sometimes merging with other fungal colonies, sometimes terminating, sometimes branching into the soil to scavenge for nutrients on their own. Each tree typically connects to several fungal partners, and each fungal partner to several trees. The result is a graph structure of bewildering complexity, with redundancy at multiple scales and dynamics running on timescales from seconds (electrical signalling) to centuries (succession of dominant fungal species).

Communication within the network is chemical and possibly electrical. Trees under insect attack release volatile organic compounds that neighbours detect, triggering pre-emptive production of defensive chemicals (tannins, phenolics, and other secondary metabolites) before the insects arrive. Babikova and colleagues (2013) showed that this signalling can travel through common mycelial networks. Bean plants connected through mycorrhizal links responded defensively to aphid attacks on neighbours, while plants without the fungal connection did not. Song and colleagues (2015) documented similar carbon-transfer and stress-signalling dynamics in Douglas fir and ponderosa pine connected through ectomycorrhizal networks.

Adamatzky (2022) has documented electrical signal spikes travelling through mycelial networks in patterns that resemble nerve impulses. The work is provocative and contested. Whether the spikes are anything like a language, as Adamatzky suggests, or whether they are basic electrochemical signalling without communicative content, remains unresolved. The ESSA framing assumes the spikes are informational in at least some loose sense.

## Common Mesh and Pattern Integrity

The story-world framing brings in two ESSA-specific terms for features of the Root Network that do not have clean Earth-science labels.

The first is the **Common Mesh**, the resource-sharing layer of the network. Nutrients, water, and chemical signals route through mycelial channels to wherever they are needed, without individual ownership and without any organism deciding the routing. The Mesh is what Simard's mother trees were thought to be taking part in when they channelled carbon to seedlings. Karst et al. (2023), discussed below, have questioned whether the Mesh in the form Simard's tradition describes is as widespread or directional as the popular account claims. The 2100 framing assumes future research has resolved this in something close to Simard's direction.

The second is **pattern integrity**. The Root Network has no concept of "self" or "individual," and asking what it wants for itself is a category error. ESSA researchers use pattern integrity to mean the health and coherence of the whole network across time. When the Network is disturbed by logging, extraction, or climate-driven shifts in soil chemistry, the disturbance can be measured as a loss of pattern integrity. The Network responds, sometimes slowly, sometimes ineffectively, to hold or restore its overall structure. Whether this response is "intentional" in any meaningful sense is exactly the question researchers at the frontier are trained to stop asking after the first few weeks.

## Timescales

Working at the Root Network frontier means accepting that the basic unit of time is wrong for human research. A "conversation" with the Network may take weeks. A "decision" may take months. A chemical query (the standard ESSA method for interacting with the substrate) requires a minimum six-week consent cycle. The cycle reflects the Network's actual processing time, not bureaucratic caution.

This temporal mismatch shapes every aspect of research method. Researchers arrive on four-month deployments and spend the first six weeks waiting for the consent cycle to complete on questions they submitted before arrival. Good fieldwork at the Root Network requires planning months before deployment and months after departure, with the bulk of analytical work happening on signal data that arrives over years.

The work attracts a particular kind of researcher and rewards a particular kind of patience. ESSA's interpretive faction has argued that the Root Network is the frontier where standard methods break down most thoroughly. There is no informant, no conversation partner, no behavioural response that maps onto human social signals. Researchers who treat the Network as a human interlocutor tend to produce work that slides into anthropomorphism and becomes unfalsifiable. Those who treat it as a non-cognitive substrate produce work that explains the chemical signalling without engaging the question that warranted recognition in the first place. Most experienced field researchers find a workable place in the middle, accepting that the Network is doing something they cannot fully describe and writing carefully about what they can document.

## Slime Mold and the Adjacent Literature

The most cited adjacent organism in the Root Network literature is slime mold (*Physarum polycephalum*), studied since the early 2000s as a model for non-neural computation. Slime mold solves mazes, optimises transport networks, and shows primitive memory, all without a single neuron. Tero and colleagues (2010) famously showed that slime mold grown on a substrate with food sources placed at major Tokyo-area cities develops a network that closely approximates the efficiency of Tokyo's rail system. The finding does not show that slime mold is intelligent in a human sense. It shows that the kind of optimisation humans associate with intelligence can be done by an organism with no centralised cognitive machinery.

The slime mold work is not directly about mycorrhizal networks, but it sits in the background of ESSA's framing because it establishes that non-neural distributed systems can solve problems requiring something functionally similar to intelligence. The Root Network is more complex, slower, and harder to study in a lab, but the same conceptual category covers both.

## Threats and Aid

The Root Network is under sustained threat from extraction industries and climate change. Logging disrupts mycelial networks severely. Old-growth forests host the most complex and resilient networks, partly because fungi and trees have had centuries to develop their connections, and partly because old-growth structure provides the stable soil conditions complex networks require. Replanted forests, even decades on, host substantially simpler networks, and some functions of old-growth networks may not be recoverable on human timescales.

ESSA's aid programs try to support network resilience through soil-condition management, reduced extraction in monitored areas, and reconnection of fragmented patches through restoration plantings. The programs are politically contested. The Sovereignty Compact has repeatedly objected to ESSA spending resources on what it calls "fungus welfare." Indigenous communities whose lands host significant Root Network presence have raised concerns about institutional capture of relationships their communities have engaged with for many generations. Researchers at the frontier are expected to take these tensions seriously, and many do, with varying degrees of success.

## The Karst Critique

The ESSA framing leans on the Simard tradition, and the 2100 assumption is that future research has resolved key empirical questions in something close to Simard's direction. The current scientific position is more contested than that framing suggests, and the contestation matters.

Karst, Jones, and Hoeksema (2023) published a critical review in *Nature Ecology & Evolution* arguing that three popular claims about the wood-wide web are insufficiently supported by current field evidence: (1) common mycorrhizal networks are widespread in forests, (2) they preferentially transfer resources from mature trees to seedlings, and (3) mature trees send warning signals to kin through them. The available field evidence, they argue, does not yet support these claims at the strength with which they have entered popular and educational discourse, and positive citation bias has amplified findings while critical and null results have been undercited.

Anyone teaching from this file should keep the Karst critique alongside it. The frontier framing is not undermined by the critique, because the framing is fictional in the sense that 2100 ESSA assumes resolved future findings. Adopters should know the underlying science is live and contested. The popular Simard-tradition account is one position in an ongoing debate, and research at the frontier in the fiction sits against a real-world background of unresolved empirical questions. The interpretive faction takes this as exactly where one would expect contested science, since the questions are genuinely difficult and standard methods do not work cleanly. The hard science faction takes it as evidence that the popular account has gotten ahead of the data and that the frontier needs methodological retrenchment. Both positions are available within the fiction, and both are defensible.

## Working at the Root Network

Field stations sit deep in old-growth forest, with research infrastructure designed to keep soil disturbance to a minimum. Researchers walk along elevated boardwalks. Sampling equipment is installed once and left across multiple deployments. The Network's response to disturbance is itself part of what researchers study, so disturbance has to be deliberate and recorded.

Standard methods include chemical signal injection (labelled compounds tracked through the network), electrical signal monitoring (Adamatzky-style probes recording spike patterns), root-system tracing (excavating small portions of root architecture to map connections), and remote-sensing of canopy and understory responses to events on the network substrate. None of these is "interviewing" the Network in any sense recognisable to human qualitative research. The Network does not have answers. It has responses, distributed across time and space, that the researcher tries to assemble into something interpretable.

Researchers at the frontier are advised to think of themselves as long-term observers of a phenomenon that may or may not be doing something cognitive, not as ethnographers. The advice is contested. Some researchers find it useful, others find it dismissive. Most find their own framing shifts over a deployment, often in directions they did not expect.

## The Science Behind the World

The 2100 framing (ESSA, the Mycelial Signal Project's 2055 confirmation, the Common Mesh and pattern integrity concepts) is fictional. The underlying claims about mycorrhizal connectivity, inter-tree resource transfer, chemical and electrical signalling, and forest-scale fungal networks rest on the work below.

The critique belongs with this file, and adopters should engage with it directly when teaching the frontier.
